### Question 1 — Answer: **B**

- **Why correct:** the qualifier is threefold — *survive the loss of one entire availability zone* + *without losing acknowledged data* + *while keeping producers writing* + *fewest configuration changes*. The `--describe` output shows every replica of each partition living on brokers 1, 2 and 3, i.e. entirely inside `az-a`, because no broker declares `broker.rack` and the controller therefore has no zone information to spread replicas with. Setting `broker.rack` (a read-only config, hence the one-at-a-time restart) and reassigning the existing partitions is the **only** option that puts a replica in a different zone. Once each partition has three replicas in three zones, losing one zone leaves two in-sync replicas, `min.insync.replicas=2` is still satisfied and `acks=all` producers keep writing.
- **Why the others are wrong:** A — `min.insync.replicas=3` on an RF=3 topic means the loss of a *single* replica blocks every `acks=all` write, so it breaks "keep producers writing" immediately, and it does nothing about the fact that all three replicas are in one zone. C — RF=6 does survive a zone loss, but it is the "right but over-engineered" option: it doubles storage and replication traffic for every partition and is a far larger change than setting one config plus one reassignment; the stem asks for the **fewest** changes. D — `unclean.leader.election.enable=true` is the classic panic switch: it lets an out-of-sync replica take leadership, which by definition discards acknowledged records — a direct violation of *without losing acknowledged data*.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** `broker.rack` is **placement** metadata. Setting it changes where *new* partitions go; the partitions you already have stay where they are until you reassign them. Both halves of answer B are required — and note this is a **read-only** config, so "without restarting brokers" would have made the question unanswerable.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-04/resources/kafka-ops-expanding-reassignment.md` and `../../study-plan/week-03/resources/kafka-design-replication.md`.

### Question 2 — Answer: **B**

- **Why correct:** the qualifier is *the FIRST action that restores writes* **without accepting the loss of acknowledged data**. The exception says it exactly: `ISR Set(3)` — one replica left — against `min.isr requirement of 2`. The topic was created with `replication.factor=2` and `min.insync.replicas=2`, i.e. **zero write fault tolerance**: losing any one of the two replicas stops writes. Bringing broker 4 back restores the second replica to the ISR and writes resume with nothing lost. Raising the topic to RF=3 before the next window is the permanent fix, so a single broker can be patched again without an outage.
- **Why the others are wrong:** A — this *does* restore writes, and it *is* a reversible dynamic topic override, which is what makes it the strongest distractor. It fails the qualifier: with `min.insync.replicas=1` an `acks=all` write is acknowledged when only the leader holds it, so any record acknowledged during the window is lost if that leader dies. §7 of the master plan states it as a rule — *"**không** hạ min.isr trong hoảng loạn"*. C — unclean election promotes a replica that is behind the high watermark; acknowledged records are discarded. D — new partitions do not help partition `audit-trail-9`, which is the one that is blocked; they inherit the same RF=2, and the partition count can never be lowered again.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** `RF=2` + `min.insync.replicas=2` is the misconfiguration the exam loves. The durability arithmetic is `RF − min.insync.replicas` = how many brokers you may lose and still write. RF 3 / min.isr 2 gives 1; RF 2 / min.isr 2 gives **0**.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-03/resources/kafka-design-replication.md` (`acks` × `min.insync.replicas` matrix) and `../../CCAAK-STUDY-PLAN.md` §7.

### Question 3 — Answer: **C**

- **Why correct:** the qualifier is *resume from the offsets they had already committed* + *no offset-translation code* + *no extra service to operate*. Cluster Linking is **byte-for-byte** replication: the mirror topic keeps the source topic's name and its offsets are identical, so a consumer failing over to the standby finds its committed offsets already meaningful. With `consumer.offset.sync.enable=true` the link syncs the consumer offsets itself. And the link runs **inside the brokers** — there is no Connect cluster, no MirrorMaker process, nothing extra on the on-call rota.
- **Why the others are wrong:** A — a stretch cluster over 900 km at 41 ms RTT with 300 ms spikes puts that latency on the critical path of every `acks=all` write; and the stem asks for a *standby cluster*, not one cluster spanning both sites. This is the option that is technically buildable and operationally indefensible. B — MM2 genuinely works and `sync.group.offsets.enabled=true` does write translated checkpoints into the destination's `__consumer_offsets`. It fails on **both** remaining qualifiers: the offsets *are* translated (conservatively, so consumers must be idempotent), and MirrorMaker 2 is a Connect-based service the team now has to run, monitor and upgrade. D — `kafka-dump-log.sh` is a log-inspection debugging tool, not a backup mechanism; a nightly export gives an RPO of up to 24 hours and replaying through a producer assigns brand-new offsets.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** the reflex line in §7 is *"Cần DR giữ nguyên offset → **Cluster Linking**"*. MM2 renames topics and translates offsets; Cluster Linking preserves both. Cluster Linking is **Confluent Platform only** — on plain Apache Kafka, MM2 is your only option and you accept translation.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-04/resources/confluent-cluster-linking.md` and `../../study-plan/week-04/resources/kafka-geo-replication-mm2.md`.

### Question 4 — Answer: **C**

- **Why correct:** three constraints, and SCRAM is the only mechanism that clears all three. *Created and revoked while the cluster is running, no restart* — SCRAM credentials live in the metadata log and are managed with `kafka-configs.sh --entity-type users`, so a service account is added or deleted at runtime. *No Kerberos KDC* — SCRAM needs none. *No readable password on the wire* — SCRAM is a challenge-response exchange (the password itself never crosses the network) and running it over `SASL_SSL` encrypts the channel as well. "Least operational machinery" also favours it: no KDC, no identity provider, no file distribution.
- **Why the others are wrong:** A — `SASL/PLAIN` credentials are static entries in the broker JAAS file, so adding or revoking one of 300 service accounts means editing a file on every broker and **restarting** them. It also puts the password in the SASL exchange itself (TLS hides it in transit, but the broker-side handling is a plaintext credential). B — `SASL/GSSAPI` is exactly the mechanism the environment cannot support: it requires a Kerberos KDC and a keytab per principal. D — the unsecured JWT login module is a development-only shortcut, and `SASL_PLAINTEXT` means the bearer token crosses the network in the clear — the security team's third constraint, violated twice.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** map each SASL mechanism to *where the credential lives*, because that is what decides whether revocation needs a restart. SCRAM → metadata log (runtime). PLAIN → JAAS file (restart). GSSAPI → KDC. OAUTHBEARER → external IdP.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-05/resources/kafka-sasl-operations.md`.

### Question 5 — Answer: **A**

- **Why correct:** the deciding facts are *each server produces its own files, the files never move*, *unreliable WAN*, *no additional cluster-wide state*, and — for the second pipeline — *must survive the loss of any single worker*. Standalone mode is a single process with local file offsets and no group membership, which is exactly right for a per-machine file tail: there is nothing cluster-wide to break when a WAN link drops. The JDBC source has the opposite requirement, so it belongs on a distributed cluster where another worker picks up its tasks.
- **Why the others are wrong:** B — a 140-worker distributed group is precisely the "additional cluster-wide state" the team ruled out: every worker joins one rebalance group, and a flapping WAN link at one store triggers rebalances that disturb the other 139. Distributing tasks across workers also cannot help when the file a task must read exists only on one specific machine. C — running the same JDBC source on three standalone workers does not give failover, it gives **triplicate records**: three independent workers each read the whole table and produce it. D — this is the correct pairing reversed, and its justification is false: a JDBC source splits its work across tables, so it commonly produces more than one task.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** the deployment-mode question is decided by *where the data lives*, not by how important the pipeline is. Data pinned to a machine (files, agents) → **standalone**. Data reachable from anywhere (a database, an API, a topic) → **distributed**, for the fault tolerance.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-06/resources/connect-user-guide-standalone-distributed.md`.

### Question 6 — Answer: **B**

- **Why correct:** the default partitioner sends a keyed record to `hash(key) % partitions`. Change the divisor and the mapping changes for most keys, so records for `userId=X` written after the change can land on a different partition from the ones already stored — the per-key ordering guarantee only ever held *within* a partition, and it is broken across the boundary. The second half matters just as much for a consumer group holding per-user state: two partitions now carry that user's history, so the state is split. And `kafka-topics.sh --alter --partitions` only ever **increases** the count.
- **Why the others are wrong:** A — the partition count cannot be lowered; the command is rejected, and Kafka never redistributes already-written records. C — no rebalance re-keys data. A consumer-group rebalance reassigns *partitions to members*; it never moves records between partitions. D — Kafka stores no "original partition" header and consumers replay in per-partition offset order only; this is an invented mechanism.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** adding partitions is one of the small set of genuinely **one-way** operations, alongside finalizing `metadata.version` and any leader elected outside the ISR. Before raising the count, ask whether the lag is really a parallelism problem (see Question 35).
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-01/resources/basic-kafka-operations-admin-cli.md` (warning that raising the partition count changes the key mapping).

### Question 7 — Answer: **A**

- **Why correct:** `1-Y · 2-Z · 3-W · 4-X`. **1 → Y** `num.replica.fetchers`: the number of fetcher threads a broker uses to pull from leaders; leaving it low means a rebuilt broker catches up slowly but the healthy leaders serve fewer, smaller fetch requests. **2 → Z** `min.insync.replicas` with `acks=all`: raising it is exactly the trade of write availability (producers block when replicas are lost) for the guarantee that acknowledged data sits on more copies. **3 → W** `linger.ms` with `batch.size`: waiting before sending produces fewer, larger requests at the cost of end-to-end latency. **4 → X** `socket.send.buffer.bytes` / `socket.receive.buffer.bytes`: more memory per connection, which is what lets a high-latency link stay filled (see Question 45).
- **Why the others are wrong:** B (`1-Z · 2-Y · 3-X · 4-W`) swaps the durability pair with the replication-thread pair and then swaps batching with socket buffers — a full inversion of both axes. C (`1-Y · 2-W · 3-Z · 4-X`) gets the fetchers right but assigns the "acknowledged data / producers block" trade-off to `linger.ms`+`batch.size` and the latency-vs-batching trade-off to `min.insync.replicas`, which have nothing to do with each other. D (`1-X · 2-Z · 3-W · 4-Y`) is correct except that it swaps the two ends: socket buffers do not control recovery speed after a restart and `num.replica.fetchers` has no bearing on per-request memory on a long link.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** read the *units* of the trade-off. "Recovery speed vs load on healthy brokers" is a **thread-count** knob. "Latency vs request size" is a **batching** knob. "Memory vs throughput on a slow link" is a **buffer** knob. "Durability vs write availability" is the only one that can lose you data.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-03/resources/kafka-broker-configs-replication-throughput.md` and `../../study-plan/week-03/resources/kafka-monitoring-tuning-signals.md`.

### Question 8 — Answer: **B**

- **Why correct:** the qualifier is *most cost-effectively* + *without adding a new service for the team to operate*. Tiered storage is the designed answer to "very long retention, cheap": hot segments stay on the broker disk, everything older moves to object storage, and retention splits in two — `retention.ms` governs the **total** (34,128,000,000 ms ≈ 395 days ≈ 13 months) while `local.retention.ms` governs the **broker disk** (172,800,000 ms = 48 h, which matches the stated read pattern exactly). It is two switches in the right order: `remote.log.storage.system.enable=true` is a **read-only** broker config, so it needs the rolling restart, and only then does the per-topic `remote.storage.enable=true` take effect. Nothing new appears on the on-call rota — object storage is not a service the Kafka team operates.
- **Why the others are wrong:** A — this works and is the "throw money at it" option: 40× the disk on every broker is the least cost-effective plan available, and the stem explicitly asks for cost-effectiveness. C — `cleanup.policy=compact` keeps the newest record per key *forever* but throws away every superseded version, which is not "13 months of history"; a compliance archive needs the record sequence, not a snapshot. It also silently disqualifies the topic from tiered storage later, since compacted topics are not supported. D — an archive cluster does meet the retention requirement, but a MirrorMaker 2 flow plus a second cluster is precisely "a new service for the team to operate", twice over.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** the two most common tiered-storage mistakes on the exam are (1) flipping only one of the two switches, and (2) forgetting `local.retention.ms`. Its default is **-2**, meaning *inherit `retention.ms`* — so enabling tiered storage and leaving local retention alone saves **zero** disk.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-02/resources/kafka-tiered-storage.md` and `../../study-plan/week-02/resources/kafka-topic-configs-overrides.md`.

### Question 9 — Answer: **B, D**

- **Why correct:** the qualifier is *relieve the pressure* **without deleting data that is still inside its retention window**, which rules out every option that frees space by deletion and leaves only the two that *move* data. **B** moves partitions to other brokers, which is the textbook cure for the skew `kafka-log-dirs.sh` just proved; the `--throttle` protects the healthy brokers during the copy, and `--verify` both confirms completion and removes the throttle configurations (see Question 29 for what happens when you skip it). **D** moves partitions between data directories on the *same* broker — a reassignment plan that names `logDirs` — which is the right move when the broker itself has spare capacity on another device and you want to avoid cross-broker network traffic entirely.
- **Why the others are wrong:** A — a small cluster-wide `log.retention.bytes` deletes segments that are still inside the retention window, on **every** topic, immediately. This is the destructive option the qualifier exists to exclude. C — `log.segment.bytes` changes how the same bytes are divided into files; it frees nothing, and larger segments actually make retention *slower* to reclaim space, because retention only deletes closed segments (Question 60). E — compaction needs keys and keeps the newest record per key, which changes the topic's semantics and discards history; on a 400 GB event-stream partition it also drives enormous cleaner I/O on a broker that is already at 94%.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** `kafka-log-dirs.sh` is **read-only** — it tells you where the bytes are and nothing else. Moving them is always `kafka-reassign-partitions.sh`, whether the destination is another broker or another directory on the same broker.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-02/resources/kafka-log-dirs-tool.md` and `../../study-plan/week-04/resources/kafka-ops-expanding-reassignment.md`.

### Question 10 — Answer: **A**

- **Why correct:** the order is **2 → 4 → 1 → 3**, and the qualifier *without any downtime and without a rolling restart* is what forces it. First widen **trust** on the brokers (step 2): a truststore holding both the old and the new CA, applied dynamically per broker, so a broker will accept a client certificate from either CA. Then confirm the **clients** are ready (step 4) — they hold new-CA certificates and their truststores also trust both CAs. Only now swap the broker **keystores** to the new certificates (step 1), one broker at a time: every client already trusts the new CA, so no handshake breaks. Finally, once nothing on either side still presents an old-CA certificate, trim the old CA out of the truststores (step 3).
- **Why the others are wrong:** B (`1 → 2 → 4 → 3`) swaps the broker keystores before anyone trusts the new CA — every client and every inter-broker connection fails the handshake the instant step 1 lands. C (`4 → 1 → 2 → 3`) issues new-CA client certificates while the brokers still trust only the old CA; with mutual TLS on the inter-broker listener, client authentication fails immediately. D (`2 → 1 → 3 → 4`) removes the old CA (step 3) *before* confirming clients have migrated (step 4), which cuts off every client still presenting an old-CA certificate — the exact downtime the stem forbids.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** rotating a CA without downtime always has the same shape: **widen trust → move the leaves → narrow trust**. Never narrow trust before you have proof that nothing still needs the old anchor. Keystore and truststore *locations* are dynamically updatable per listener, which is what makes the no-restart path possible at all — one practical caveat noted in [`../../study-plan/VALIDATION.md`](../../study-plan/VALIDATION.md) (Lab 5.2) is that changing only the `location` and letting the password fall back to the generic config is documented behaviour but has not been proven on a live cluster in this repo.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-05/resources/confluent-dynamic-config-cert-rotation.md` and `../../study-plan/week-05/resources/kafka-ssl-operations.md`.

### Question 11 — Answer: **D**

- **Why correct:** two qualifiers do the work — *without restarting any broker* and *without pushing extra fetch load onto the healthy brokers*. Broker 7 is the **follower** that has to pull 1,900 partitions; `num.replica.fetchers` is the size of *its* fetcher pool, so raising it only on broker 7 parallelises exactly the work that is slow. The config's update mode is `cluster-wide`, which per the docs also means *"may be updated as a per-broker value"* — so `kafka-configs.sh --entity-type brokers --entity-name 7` applies it live, and `--delete-config` removes it afterwards. Fully reversible, no restart, no extra fetching by anyone else.
- **Why the others are wrong:** A — lowering `replica.lag.time.max.ms` to 5000 makes `UnderReplicatedPartitions` *look* better by evicting the lagging replicas from the ISR faster. It speeds up nothing, and it shrinks the ISR of every partition on a cluster that is already degraded. Treating a metric by changing its threshold is the trap. B — raising `num.replica.fetchers` as a **cluster-wide default** does work, and this is the strongest distractor; it fails the second qualifier, because it also multiplies the fetcher threads on the five healthy brokers, each of which then issues more fetch requests against leaders whose produce latency is already at the SLA edge. C — `replica.fetch.max.bytes` in `server.properties` is a static change requiring a roll of all brokers, which the first qualifier forbids.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** when only one broker is behind, the fix belongs on **that broker**. A per-broker dynamic override is the cheapest, most reversible instrument in the KRaft toolbox — and remember that `num.replica.fetchers` describes the fetching side, so it is set on the follower, never on the leader.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-03/resources/kafka-broker-configs-replication-throughput.md` and `../../study-plan/week-02/resources/confluent-dynamic-config-precedence.md` (update modes; per-broker dynamic override).

### Question 12 — Answer: **C, E**

- **Why correct:** **C** — with RF=3 and `min.insync.replicas=3`, the ISR is at the minimum the moment everything is healthy. The first replica to leave the ISR — for any reason, including a GC pause — drops it to 2 and every `acks=all` producer is rejected with `NotEnoughReplicasException`. The topic has **no write fault tolerance at all**, which is the opposite of what the architect thinks they bought. **E** — a rolling restart deliberately takes one broker down at a time, so during every single step one replica is absent and the partitions led on that broker are below min ISR. Routine maintenance becomes a write outage.
- **Why the others are wrong:** A — `unclean.leader.election.enable` stays `false`; nothing about `min.insync.replicas` changes it, and Kafka never silently enables data loss to compensate for a strict setting. B — consumers keep reading. `min.insync.replicas` gates the **write** path; a fetch below the high watermark is served normally. What *does* happen under the strict-min-ISR rule is that the high watermark stops advancing, so consumers see no *new* data — but existing committed data is still readable, which is not what the option claims. D — backwards: the ISR shrinks exactly as freely as before. `min.insync.replicas` does not prevent a replica falling out; it only decides what happens to writes when one does, which is why `UnderReplicatedPartitions` behaves identically.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** `replication.factor` is *how many copies exist*; `min.insync.replicas` is *how many must be present to accept a write*. The gap between them is your tolerance budget. Setting them equal spends the entire budget before anything has gone wrong.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-03/resources/kafka-design-replication.md` (`acks` × `min.insync.replicas`) and `../../study-plan/week-03/resources/kafka-eligible-leader-replicas.md` (strict min ISR and the high watermark).

### Question 13 — Answer: **C**

- **Why correct:** the status output and the consumer group tell one consistent story. `tasks.max` is a **ceiling**, not an instruction — and most sink connectors return the full number requested, so twelve tasks really were created and really are `RUNNING`. Each sink task is a consumer in `connect-s3-orders`; twelve consumers in a group can share at most six partitions, so six members get one partition each and six get nothing. The six idle tasks consume worker resources and change throughput by zero. Setting `tasks.max` back to 6 restores the sane state, and raising the partition count is the only thing that would add real parallelism — at the price of the irreversibility spelled out in Question 6.
- **Why the others are wrong:** A — the formula `min(tasks.max, partitions)` is a widespread misconception and is **wrong**; the status output is not an artefact, those twelve tasks exist. There is also no `?includeTasks=true` on a connector *restart* that "clears" tasks — that parameter restarts tasks along with the connector, it does not delete them. B — cooperative rebalancing had already completed; the group shows a stable assignment of six owners and six non-owners, not a pending one. `scheduled.rebalance.max.delay.ms` governs how long the leader waits for a **departed worker** to return (Question 59) and cannot conjure partitions that do not exist. D — `max.poll.records` changes how many records a task fetches per `poll()`; it cannot give a partition to a consumer that owns none.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** sink parallelism is capped by **partitions**, never by `tasks.max`. Source parallelism is capped by whatever the connector can split (tables, files, shards). Setting `tasks.max` above that ceiling produces idle tasks, not throughput.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-06/resources/connect-cluster-rebalance-kip415-kip891.md` (`tasks.max` is a ceiling; the `min(tasks.max, partitions)` formula is wrong).

### Question 14 — Answer: **D**

- **Why correct:** the order is **2 → 4 → 1 → 3**. Start the new brokers first (step 2) — they join with new node ids and the same `cluster.id`, and until the cluster actually lists five brokers you cannot generate a plan that references brokers 4 and 5. Then `--generate` (step 4) against the full broker list, keeping the printed **current** assignment: that printout is your rollback plan and it exists only at this moment. Then `--execute` with a `--throttle` (step 1) so the copy does not starve normal traffic. Finally `--verify` (step 3), which both reports completion and **removes the throttle configurations** it installed.
- **Why the others are wrong:** A (`4 → 2 → 1 → 3`) generates the plan before the new brokers exist, so `--broker-list "1,2,3,4,5"` names brokers the cluster has never seen. B (`2 → 1 → 4 → 3`) executes a plan that has not been generated yet — there is no `plan.json` to pass to `--execute`. C (`2 → 4 → 3 → 1`) verifies before executing, which reports "not started" and leaves the actual move as the last step, with the throttle still in place and nothing to remove it.

### Question 31 — Answer: **C**

### Question 46 — Answer: **C**

- **Why correct:** the qualifier is *fires **before** unread records are deleted by retention*. Lag and lead measure distances to opposite ends of the log. **Lag** is the distance from the consumer's position to the **log end offset** — how far behind the head it is, which says nothing about how close the **tail** is. **`records-lead-min`** is the distance from the consumer's position to the **log start offset**, and the log start offset is exactly what retention advances. As the 24-hour retention boundary catches up with a slow nightly batch, `records-lead-min` falls toward 0; when it reaches 0, records are being deleted unread. That is the early warning, and it is a consumer-side client metric (`kafka.consumer:type=consumer-fetch-manager-metrics`).
- **Why the others are wrong:** A — `records-lag-max` with a fixed threshold of a million records is a guess with no relationship to retention: a million records might be twenty minutes of headroom on a busy day and twelve hours on a quiet one, so the alert fires at the wrong time in both directions. B — the `LAG` column measures the same quantity as A, from committed offsets rather than fetch position (and therefore one commit interval stale); scraping it more often does not change what it measures. D — `UnderMinIsrPartitionCount` reports that `|ISR| < min.insync.replicas`, i.e. that `acks=all` **writes** are blocked. It is a producer-availability signal and has no relationship to retention deleting unread records.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** "am I behind?" is **lag**; "am I about to lose data?" is **lead**. They are different metrics with different thresholds, and only one of them can warn you before the data is gone. Note also that neither works for a consumer using `assign()` rather than `subscribe()` — no group, no server-side lag.

### Question 51 — Answer: **B**

- **Why correct:** a follower serves fetch requests only up to **its own high watermark**, and the high watermark marks records that every in-sync replica has already acknowledged — the definition of committed. A follower learns that the high watermark has advanced on its next fetch round, which is where the ~200 ms comes from. So the consumer is reading records that are just as committed as the leader's, only slightly older. Nothing about durability, ordering or the visibility rule changes; follower fetching trades **freshness** for **cross-zone cost**, and that is the whole of the trade.
- **Why the others are wrong:** A — this describes reading *uncommitted* data, which cannot happen: no replica, leader or follower, serves records above its high watermark. If it could, follower fetching would be unusable and the feature would not exist. C — `min.insync.replicas` is evaluated on the **write** path at the leader; a follower does not apply it locally and does not withhold reads waiting for other replicas. D — lowering `replica.lag.time.max.ms` from 30000 to 5000 does not make a follower fetch more often; it only shortens how long a lagging replica may stay in the ISR before being evicted. Applied here it would evict the very followers the consumers are reading from, taking them out of eligibility (only in-sync replicas may serve follower fetches) and making things worse.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** the high watermark is the visibility boundary for **every** replica, which is exactly why follower fetching is safe. Keep the two clocks apart: the high watermark decides *what may be read*; `replica.lag.time.max.ms` decides *who stays in the ISR*. Lowering the second never improves the first.

The number in the stem points squarely at `scheduled.rebalance.max.delay.ms`.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** "worker restarted, tasks did not move for exactly five minutes" is a configured behaviour, not a bug — recognise **300000 ms** on sight. And keep the two rebalance facts together: a **worker** failure triggers a rebalance; a **task** failure does not.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-06/resources/connect-cluster-rebalance-kip415-kip891.md` and `../../study-plan/week-06/resources/connect-worker-configs-4.3.md` (`scheduled.rebalance.max.delay.ms` 300000).

### Question 60 — Answer: **D**

- **Why correct:** retention deletes whole **closed** segments and never touches the active one, so the unit of deletion is a segment, not a record. The arithmetic in the stem is the whole answer: `segment.bytes` is the 1 GiB default, `segment.ms` is not set on the topic, and each partition receives about 12 MB per hour — so a segment takes roughly `1024 ÷ 12 ≈ 85 hours` to fill and roll. Eighteen hours in, every partition is still writing into its first active segment, nothing has closed, and therefore nothing is eligible for deletion regardless of `retention.ms=3600000`. The fix is to make segments roll on a schedule the retention can act on: set `segment.ms` on the topic, or lower `segment.bytes`.
- **Why the others are wrong:** A — `log.retention.check.interval.ms` is 300,000 ms (5 minutes), so the checker has run over two hundred times in eighteen hours. It has been running and finding nothing to delete. B — the precedence claim is inverted: a topic override **wins** over a broker-level value (Question 36), so if a topic override existed it would be the one in force. The stem says `retention.ms` was set on `sensor-raw`, i.e. at topic level already. C — invented. Time-based deletion is what `cleanup.policy=delete` does; `compact` adds compaction on top and `compact,delete` runs both. Deletion does not require compaction to be enabled.
- 🧠 **Key point / trap:** §7 states the reflex — *"retention đặt 1 giờ mà data vẫn còn → segment active chưa đóng → hạ `segment.ms` hoặc `segment.bytes`"*. Retention is a promise about a **lower bound**, never an upper one: Kafka guarantees it will keep data at least that long, not that it will delete it promptly. One 4.3 detail to remember before writing a test: the minimum permitted `segment.bytes` rose from 14 bytes to **1 MiB**, so old scripts that set tiny segments are now rejected.
- 📎 Source: `../../study-plan/week-02/resources/kafka-topic-configs-overrides.md` and `../../study-plan/week-02/resources/kafka-broker-configs-storage-threads.md` (`log.segment.bytes` 1 GiB, `log.retention.check.interval.ms` 300000).
